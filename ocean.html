Epipelagic · 0–40 m

Sunlit reef

Sunlight still reaches the sand. Reef-building corals depend on algae living in their tissue, so they only grow where light does, and the reef stops where the shelf drops away.

Epipelagic · 40–200 m

The blue

Past the drop-off the floor falls out of sight. Red light is gone in the first few tens of metres; by 200 m only about one percent of surface light remains.

Sardine bait ball
Sardinops sagax
Giant manta ray
Mobula birostris
Humpback whale
Megaptera novaeangliae

Mesopelagic · 200–1,000 m

Twilight zone

Too dim for photosynthesis, bright enough to be seen. Most animals here make their own light. Every night lanternfish rise toward the surface to feed, the largest daily migration on Earth.

Helmet jellyfish
Periphylla periphylla
Atolla jellyfish
Atolla wyvillei
Lanternfish
Family Myctophidae

Bathypelagic · 1,000–4,000 m

Midnight zone

No sunlight reaches this far. The only light is bioluminescence, used to lure prey, find mates and startle predators. Pressure passes 100 atmospheres and the water holds near 4 °C.

Humpback anglerfish
Melanocetus johnsonii
Giant siphonophore
Praya dubia

Abyssopelagic · 4,000 m and below

Abyssal plain

The plain sits close to 2 °C. At the vents, seawater heated inside the crust comes back out at over 350 °C, and whole communities live on chemical energy instead of sunlight.

Hydrothermal chimneys
